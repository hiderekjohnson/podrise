Huberman Lab

45 min  ·  Mar 13, 2026

The Science of Dopamine, Motivation & Drive

Key Takeaways

 Cold exposure spikes dopamine 250% with no crash afterward — unlike food, substances, or social media which all cause a rebound dip below baseline.
Layering rewards kills both. Coffee while scrolling, music while working out — stacking dopamine sources dulls each one over time.
Anticipation is the drug, not the reward. Dopamine peaks before you get what you want — systems built around ongoing anticipation sustain motivation far longer.

Andrew Huberman and Dr. Anna Lembke break down what dopamine actually does — and doesn't do. The episode covers why the modern environment is essentially a dopamine trap, the difference between dopamine as reward vs. motivation signal, and the behavioral tools to protect your baseline. Cold exposure protocols, dopamine fasting, and why the most motivated people find pleasure in the effort itself — not the outcome.

The Simplest Way To Make $1M In 2026

Key Takeaways

 Join a rocket ship, don't build one. Sam's wife made her first million as employee #3000 at Airbnb through stock options — the best move is finding a company already on a steep trajectory and getting on board early.
Boring niches have the biggest upside. SendCutSend ($100M+ ARR, bootstrapped, barely anyone's heard of it) thrives in an unsexy category where smart operators face zero cool-founder competition.
Vertical AI is the real opportunity. Harvey has half the top 100 US law firms already — the winners aren't building general AI, they're wrapping it tightly around one specific, high-value workflow.

Sam kicks off with the story of his wife Sarah — employee #3000 at Airbnb, first million made through stock options. That story anchors the episode: the fastest path to $1M isn't always starting a company, it's joining the right one early. From there, Sam and Shaan walk through their updated "Sarah's List" — ten companies they'd genuinely join or start today.

Standouts include Zurutech (AI-designed, factory-built homes at a fraction of traditional cost), Varda Space Industries (manufacturing materials in microgravity that are physically impossible to produce on Earth), and Suno — an AI music platform at $300M ARR and 2M users that lets non-musicians make professional-quality tracks.

The episode also covers the SaaS valuation dip — HubSpot and peers represent a generational buying opportunity as AI gets layered in — and closes with deep dives on Harvey (AI for law), SemiAnalysis (semiconductor research becoming a Moody's-style ratings business), and SendCutSend (bootstrapped custom parts manufacturing, $100M+ ARR).

In Our Time

45 min  ·  Mar 10, 2026

Henry IV Part 1

Key Takeaways

 Falstaff was renamed to avoid a political scandal — originally Sir John Oldcastle, a Protestant martyr. His descendants complained. Shakespeare swapped the name and kept everything else intact.
Hal's redemption arc is entirely calculated. His Act 1 soliloquy reveals he's been performing recklessness deliberately — so the transformation hits harder. It's cold strategy, not spontaneous growth.
History plays were political cover. In the 1590s, staging questions of succession through historical allegory was the only safe way to address what everyone was actually anxious about: Elizabeth I had no named heir.

Melvyn Bragg and a panel of Shakespeare scholars dig into Henry IV Part 1 — the play's tensions between legitimacy and rebellion, Prince Hal's duality, and Falstaff's function as a comic counterweight to heroism. Emma Smith sets up the structural genius: Hal exists simultaneously in the taverns of East Cheap and the court of Westminster, and Shakespeare uses that split to interrogate what leadership actually requires.

The panel debates whether the play is a coming-of-age story or something more unsettling. Lucy Munro argues Hotspur's unconditional obsession with honor makes him the more sympathetic figure even as it leads directly to his death. The Battle of Shrewsbury is the climax — Hal earns his legitimacy through chivalric performance while Falstaff dismantles the concept of honor entirely in his famous soliloquy, delivered right before the fighting starts.
