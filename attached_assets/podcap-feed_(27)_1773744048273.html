Ep 689 — This guy names billion dollar brands for a living, here's his exact 3-step formula 14h ago
The guy with the secret to billion-dollar brand names
Key Takeaways
  • A brand's name is its most-used asset. 'Swiffer' beat 'ProMop' because it was more processing-fluent and surprising — that single naming decision drove the product's market success.
  • Choosing a name that feels safe usually means choosing a name that fails to stand out. 'Windsurf' replacing 'Codium' significantly boosted brand visibility without changing a single feature.
  • The process: generate thousands of candidates across linguistic and thematic categories, then filter for alignment with both the product and the consumer's mental model.
  • Present potential names in realistic scenarios so clients can feel their impact — context makes names believable, especially for clients unfamiliar with creative decisions.
"The most frequently used part of your brand isn't your logo, your tagline, or your colour palette. It's your name."
— David (brand naming strategist)
Episode Recap

David began by challenging the notion that a company's name isn't important, arguing that a good name is the most frequently used part of a brand and can provide a strategic advantage. He recounted the story of how the Swiffer name was chosen over ProMop, demonstrating how a name can transform market perception and success.

He emphasised that a great name should be original, processing fluent, and surprising, sharing the example of renaming Codium to Windsurf, which significantly boosted the brand's visibility. David also highlighted the importance of not choosing comfortable or popular names, as they often fail to stand out.

The conversation delved into the process of finding the right name, where David explained the importance of understanding both the product and the consumer's needs. He detailed how his team generates thousands of name candidates by exploring various linguistic and thematic categories.

David discussed the 'comfort trap' in naming and the need for courage in choosing names that might initially seem polarising but have the potential to be memorable and impactful. He also shared insights into how his firm uses software and cognitive science principles to evaluate the effectiveness of names.

The episode also covered the significance of presenting names in a context that makes them believable, such as showcasing them in realistic scenarios to clients. This helps clients, who may not be used to making creative decisions, see the potential value of a name.

David concluded by offering advice on managing creative teams, encouraging leaders to focus on leading rather than managing and to foster an environment where creativity can thrive without immediate judgment. He also mentioned the value of giving creative teams permission to fail as part of the creative process.

Ep 512 — Stoic advice for the problems we all face 8h ago
The price of profit Ryan Holiday won't pay
Key Takeaways
  • Diversifying workouts with strength training enhances both physical and mental wellness — Holiday's personal shift was supported by Tonal's home gym system.
  • Stoicism does not advocate isolation — even Marcus Aurelius valued deep friendships and community, countering the misconception that Stoics are cold and detached.
  • In business, prioritising principles over profit aligns with Stoic ethics — Holiday's time at American Apparel saw principled decisions made at real financial cost.
  • Gandhi and MLK used Stoic principles as proof that moral example is a practical strategic tool — non-violent resistance is the most disciplined form of action.
"You can always make more money. You cannot always get your integrity back."
— Ryan Holiday
Episode Recap

Holiday opens with something unexpected: strength training. His recent shift toward more weight work — supported by Tonal — is framed as a Stoic act in itself: caring for your vessel so your mind can do its work. It's a small example of what the episode keeps returning to: philosophy that lives in actions, not ideas.

He addresses a question he fielded at a recent San Diego talk about Marcus Aurelius and friendship. The misconception that Stoics are cold and isolated is widespread. Holiday is direct — Aurelius had deep friendships and was deeply community-oriented. The philosophy was never about detachment from people. It was about detachment from outcomes.

The most pointed section draws on his time at American Apparel — a company run by someone with remarkable commercial instincts and almost no principles — to argue that Stoicism provides a practical framework for deciding what you won't do, regardless of what it costs. The Gandhi and MLK section extends this into activism: non-violent resistance isn't passive, it's the most disciplined form of action there is.

Holiday closes by reframing the whole episode's thread: Stoicism isn't a philosophy of endurance. It's a philosophy of clarity — about what matters, what doesn't, and what you'll sacrifice for the difference.
