The right brand name creates a strategic moat — here's the 3-step formula that named Swiffer, BlackBerry, and Impossible Burger

Home › Podcasts › My First Million

Mar 13, 2026 1 hr 0 min Sam Parr & Shaan Puri with David Placek

Key takeaways

  • A brand name is the most frequently used part of any brand — more than the logo, the tagline, or the product itself. Swiffer beat ProMop not because the product was better, but because the name shifted perception entirely.
  • The three criteria for a powerful name: original (nothing else sounds like it), processing-fluent (easy to say and remember), and surprising (creates a moment of pause). A name that hits all three is almost impossible to ignore.
  • Choosing a safe, comfortable name is the most common and most costly naming mistake. Comfortable names blend in. "Windsurf" replaced "Codium" and drove a measurable spike in brand awareness within weeks.
  • David's team generates thousands of name candidates before presenting any — exploring linguistic roots, thematic categories, and cognitive science principles to evaluate each one's memorability before a human sees it.
  • The "comfort trap" in naming means most founding teams will reject the best name in the room because it feels risky. The names that feel polarizing in a boardroom are often the ones that win in the market.
  • BlackBerry was chosen because it felt tactile and organic — a deliberate contrast to the cold, corporate names dominating tech at the time. The surprise of the word was the point.

Episode recap

David Placek opened by making the case that most founders treat naming as an afterthought — something to resolve after the product is built, the deck is done, and the funding is closed. His argument: the name is the most-used piece of your brand. It appears in every conversation, every press mention, every investor email, every customer referral. Getting it wrong is expensive in a way that's hard to measure until it's too late.

The Swiffer story was his clearest example. P&G's internal name for the product was ProMop. It described what the product did. It was functional, accurate, and instantly forgettable. Swiffer did none of those things — it sounded like movement, like cleaning, like something slightly fun. It was original, processing-fluent, and surprising. It won every focus group. ProMop never had a chance.

David's framework for naming has three criteria. A name must be original — nothing in the market should sound like it. It must be processing-fluent — easy to say, easy to spell, easy to remember. And it must be surprising — it should create a brief moment of cognitive pause that makes the brain pay attention. A name that hits all three is extremely rare. His firm generates thousands of candidates using linguistic databases and cognitive science tools before narrowing to a shortlist of ten.

The Windsurf story — the renaming of Codium — was his most recent example. Codium was a defensible, category-appropriate name for an AI coding tool. It described the function. It was safe. Windsurf replaced it. The name carried speed, movement, and a physical quality that made it feel different from every other AI tool. Traffic, press mentions, and brand recall measurably improved within weeks of the rename.

Sam pushed back on how you get internal alignment on a name that feels risky. David's answer was direct: you don't. The best name in the room will always make someone uncomfortable. The job of the naming process isn't to find a name everyone loves — it's to find the name the market will remember. Those are almost never the same name.

Guest

D

David Placek

Founder, Lexicon Branding

David Placek founded Lexicon Branding in 1982 and has spent four decades naming some of the most recognized products in the world — including Swiffer, BlackBerry, Dasani, Pentium, and Impossible Burger. His methodology draws on cognitive science, linguistics, and consumer psychology to find names that are original, processing-fluent, and surprising. He argues that the name is the single most underinvested asset in any brand.
